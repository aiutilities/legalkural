# Rent Is Business. Living Is Residence.

## When the Madras High Court asked what a hostel really is

A hostel owner earns rent.

That fact made the municipal authorities see a commercial establishment.

But the residents saw something else.

They returned after work, slept in their rooms, bathed, ate, washed their clothes and continued their daily lives there.

The Madras High Court therefore asked a simple but important question:

> Should a building be classified by how its owner earns money, or by how its occupants actually use it?

The Court chose actual use.

# The Story

Working men and women often move to cities for employment.

Many cannot afford an independent flat, apartment or house.

They therefore share rooms in hostels or paying-guest accommodation.

The rooms may be small.

The kitchen may be common.

Several people may share the same space.

But after work, that is where they return.

That is where they sleep.

That is where they eat, bathe, wash clothes and live.

A group of hostel owners in Chennai and Coimbatore found that their properties had been classified or reclassified under commercial tariffs.

This led to higher property tax, water tax, water charges and related demands.

The authorities looked at the hostel owners and saw businesses earning rent.

The hostel owners asked the Court to look instead at the residents.

Their argument was simple:

> The owner may run a business, but the occupant is still using the room as a residence.

---

# What Led to the Case?

The municipal and utility authorities raised commercial-tariff demands against several hostel properties.

Some properties had earlier been assessed as residential.

The petitioners challenged:

- property-tax demands;
- water-tax and water-charge demands;
- commercial reclassification on municipal portals;
- recovery notices; and
- related electricity tariff consequences.

The hostel owners also complained that the classification had been changed without prior notice or an opportunity to explain the actual use of their properties.

---

# What the Hostel Owners Argued

The petitioners said their occupants were mainly working men and women from economically weaker and lower-middle-income groups.

These residents shared rooms because independent accommodation was unaffordable.

Inside the hostel, they performed ordinary residential activities:

- sleeping;
- bathing;
- eating;
- washing clothes;
- using toilets;
- using common kitchens; and
- returning after their daily work.

The petitioners accepted that operating a hostel could be a business and that rental income could be treated accordingly for income-tax purposes.

But they argued that the owner’s business did not change the residents’ use of the rooms.

They also argued that higher commercial tariffs would ultimately be passed on to the inmates.

---

# What the Authorities Argued

The authorities said the petitioners were conducting the business of letting hostel rooms.

They relied on regulations and statutes that referred to private hostels, lodging houses and commercial premises.

They also argued that:

- a statutory appeal was available;
- the petitioners should have approached the Taxation Appeals Committee;
- the High Court should not directly entertain the writ petitions; and
- residential treatment could cause revenue loss to municipal and utility bodies.

---

# The Two Questions Before the Court

The Court framed two central questions.

## Question 1

Should the hostel properties be treated as commercial premises by looking at the business conducted by the owners?

## Question 2

Could the petitioners approach the High Court directly despite the availability of a statutory appeal, particularly when natural justice was alleged to have been violated?

---

# How the Judge Reasoned

The judgment followed a clear reasoning pattern.

## 1. Separate the owner from the occupant

The owner operated a business and received rent.

The occupant used the room for daily living.

The Court treated these as two separate questions.

Business income describes the owner’s economic activity.

It does not automatically describe the physical use of the building.

## 2. Examine what actually happens inside the property

The Court looked at the activities conducted by the hostel inmates.

They slept there.

They ate there.

They bathed there.

They washed clothes there.

They used the premises as accommodation after work.

These were residential activities.

## 3. Compare hostels with rented apartments

The Court used an apartment example.

An apartment owner may rent flats and earn income.

If tenants use those flats as homes, the flats remain residential.

The Court saw no reason to apply a different yardstick merely because several working people shared a hostel room.

The legal nature of sleeping and living did not change because the accommodation was shared.

## 4. Apply the statutory definition of residence

The relevant municipal laws defined residence by reference to using a house, hut or part of it as a sleeping apartment.

The hostel rooms were used precisely in that manner.

The Court therefore found statutory support for residential classification.

## 5. View the issue from the recipient’s perspective

The judgment relied on an earlier Madras High Court decision concerning GST treatment of hostel accommodation.

That decision had held that the nature of the service should be examined from the perspective of the person receiving and using the accommodation.

The same principle was applied here.

The relevant question was not merely:

> Is the owner running a business?

It was:

> What is the resident doing inside the premises?

## 6. Consider the social burden

The Court also examined who would ultimately bear the higher tax.

The hostel owner could pass the increased cost to the residents.

Those residents often chose shared accommodation because they could not afford independent housing.

The Court considered it discriminatory to impose higher commercial charges on people carrying out the same residential activities as those living in apartments or houses.

## 7. Test procedural fairness

The authorities did not produce documentary evidence proving that prior notice had been issued before changing the properties from residential to commercial tariffs.

The petitioners had not been given an opportunity to explain the actual use of the premises.

The Court therefore found a violation of natural justice.

---

# Why the High Court Entertained the Case

Ordinarily, courts may ask a person to use the statutory appeal process before filing a writ petition.

But that rule has exceptions.

Here, the dispute involved:

- interpretation of statutory definitions;
- a substantial legal question;
- the correct method of property classification; and
- an alleged violation of natural justice.

The Court therefore held that the availability of an appeal did not prevent it from exercising jurisdiction under Article 226.

---

# The Decision

The High Court held that the hostel activities in these cases were residential in nature.

It directed that residential tariffs should apply to:

- property tax;
- water tax;
- water charges; and
- electricity charges connected to the classification.

The impugned demand notices were quashed.

All the writ petitions were allowed.

There was no order as to costs.

---

# An Important Limitation

The Court did not declare that every hostel in Tamil Nadu must automatically receive residential classification.

The judgment expressly required factual verification.

A hostel seeking similar treatment must establish that its occupants genuinely use the rooms for residential activities.

A property or room used for an independent commercial activity may still receive commercial classification.

The ruling is therefore based on function, not label.

---

# The Judge’s Pattern

The judgment can be mapped into seven steps:

```text
Identify the real user
        ↓
Identify the actual activity
        ↓
Separate ownership from usage
        ↓
Apply the statutory definition
        ↓
Compare similar situations equally
        ↓
Verify notice and hearing
        ↓
Grant fact-specific relief
```

This is the reusable judicial pattern in the case.

# Legal Terms in Plain Language

## Writ Petition

A request asking the High Court to examine whether a public authority acted illegally, unfairly or beyond its powers.

## Article 226

The constitutional power of a High Court to issue directions and protect legal and fundamental rights.

## Natural Justice

The basic rule that a person should receive notice and a fair chance to respond before an adverse decision is taken.

## Alternative Remedy

Another legal process, such as a statutory appeal, that may ordinarily be used before approaching the High Court.

## Commercial Tariff

A higher rate applied when property or services are classified as being used for business or commercial purposes.

## Residential Tariff

A rate applied when property is used as a home or dwelling.

## Impugned Notice

The official notice being challenged before the Court.

## Quashed

Cancelled by the Court because it was unlawful or procedurally defective.

# For the Citizen

The judgment says that authorities should look at reality.

A shared hostel room may still be a person’s home.

The fact that the owner earns rent does not automatically make every activity inside the building commercial.

Before imposing a higher tariff, the authority must inspect the real use and give the affected person a chance to explain.

---

# For the Law Student

The case demonstrates the interaction between:

- functional statutory interpretation;
- purposive interpretation;
- Article 14 equality reasoning;
- Article 226 writ jurisdiction;
- exceptions to the alternative-remedy rule;
- natural justice; and
- fact-specific administrative classification.

The ratio rests on actual use by the recipient rather than the commercial identity of the provider.

---

# For the Lawyer

The judgment offers a reusable challenge structure for adverse property classification:

1. Establish the occupants’ actual activity.
2. Distinguish the owner’s income character from property use.
3. Identify the applicable statutory definition.
4. Produce evidence of residential occupation.
5. Challenge the absence of notice and hearing.
6. Invoke the natural-justice exception to alternative remedy.
7. Seek fact-specific reassessment rather than automatic universal classification.

The judgment must be used carefully because the Court limited its application to premises demonstrating comparable residential use.

---

# What Evidence Mattered?

This was not a witness-heavy trial.

The important material included:

- the impugned tax and recovery notices;
- online municipal reclassification records;
- statutory definitions of residence;
- water-board regulations;
- the earlier GST judgment;
- the nature of the activities performed by occupants; and
- the absence of documentary proof of prior notice.

An important lesson from the case is that missing evidence can itself become decisive.

The respondents’ inability to prove prior communication supported the natural-justice finding.

---

# Why the Case Took Time

The common order covered a large batch of writ petitions involving different hostel owners, properties, municipal bodies and utility authorities.

The Court had to examine:

- several statutes;
- municipal regulations;
- constitutional principles;
- earlier GST litigation;
- multiple demand notices; and
- both classification and procedural issues.

The order was reserved on 29 August 2025 and pronounced on 7 November 2025.

# The Legal Kural Principle

## Usage Decides

The deepest lesson is not limited to hostels.

Whenever law classifies property, services or activity, labels may conceal reality.

The name of the establishment is relevant.

The business of the owner is relevant.

But neither should replace proof of what actually happens there.

> **Profit may describe the owner.**
>
> **Purpose must describe the place.**

---

# Final Takeaway

The judgment did not deny that hostel owners conduct business.

It held that business ownership and residential usage can exist at the same time.

The owner earns.

The occupant lives.

The law must recognise both without confusing one for the other.
