# 004 — Judgment Grammar

## Status

`CANONICAL — NORMATIVE`

## Purpose

Judgment grammar defines the legal roles and permitted relationships used to
turn a judgment into structured artifacts and an explanatory article. It
prevents narrative fluency from collapsing legally distinct concepts.

## Core Sequence

The canonical reasoning sequence is:

`metadata → timeline → facts → issues → evidence → law → reasoning → decision`

The sequence is analytical, not merely chronological. Each downstream element
must be supported by the judgment and linked to its upstream basis.

## Grammar Elements

### Metadata

Court, bench, judge, case number, parties, decision date, source identity and
procedural posture.

### Timeline

Source-supported events and procedural steps in date order. Unknown dates stay
unknown.

### Facts

Material facts accepted, recorded or relied upon by the Court. Allegations and
submissions must be separately attributed.

### Issues

Questions the Court actually considered or had to decide. An editor must not
invent a broader policy question.

### Evidence and Record

Documents, notices, testimony, admissions and other material described in the
judgment, together with the role the Court assigned to them.

### Law

Statutory provisions, rules, precedents and legal tests identified by the
judgment. A citation is not automatically a rule adopted by the Court.

### Reasoning

The ordered chain connecting material facts and governing law to findings and
the holding. Counterarguments and rejected reasoning remain attributed.

### Decision

Holding, disposition, operative directions, relief, costs and explicit limits.
The decision must not be expanded beyond the case or class stated by the Court.

## Attribution Types

Every substantive proposition must be classifiable as one of:

- procedural record;
- party allegation;
- party submission;
- evidence or document described;
- governing law or cited authority;
- Court observation;
- Court finding;
- holding;
- operative order; or
- editorial explanation derived from the foregoing.

## Page References

Structured artifacts must retain source page references wherever the judgment
supports them. An article may combine references for readability but must not
discard the ability to trace a claim to its source.

## Conflict and Silence

When sources conflict, record the conflict and the Court's resolution, if any.
When the judgment is silent, preserve silence. Do not infer motive, credibility,
intent, commercial effect or wider precedent without source support.

## Article Transformation

Article prose may reorder explanation for readers only if it preserves this
grammar. The article must clearly distinguish what happened, what was argued,
what law applied, how the Court reasoned, what it decided and the limits of the
result.
